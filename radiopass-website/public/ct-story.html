01 — Scanner generations

Forty years,
four geometries.

A · First generation

Translate–rotate, pencil beam

A single pencil beam and one detector step sideways across the patient, then rotate. Minutes per slice.

B · Second generation

Translate–rotate, narrow fan

A small fan and a short detector row cover more tissue per translation. Fewer rotation steps, faster scans.

C · Third generation

Rotate–rotate

A wide fan covers the whole patient. Tube and detector arc rotate together — the geometry used by modern scanners.

D · Fourth generation

Rotate–stationary ring

A complete stationary ring of detectors; only the tube rotates inside it. Costly, and largely abandoned.

02 — Shaping the beam

Filter first,
then collimate.

The patient is thinner at the edges than the middle. A bow-tie filter — thick at its edges, thin at its centre — pre-attenuates the periphery of the fan, so the detectors see a flatter, more uniform signal and skin dose falls.

The collimator then trims the beam along z, defining beam width and limiting dose.
Watch the profile: strong attenuation across the filter’s wings, gentle at its centre — the mirror image of the patient’s own attenuation.

03 — Multi-slice detector arrays

Rows decide
the slices.

In multi-slice CT the detector is a 2-D mosaic. How its rows are sized along z defines the array type — and, with the collimation, the slice thickness.

Matrix — every row the same width.
Adaptive — fine rows at the centre, progressively wider towards the edges.
Hybrid — a fine central set inside uniform outer rows.
In-plane, the fan spans the patient in x–y; side-on, collimation sets beam thickness, detector configuration sets slice thickness.
04 — Helical acquisition

The beam
draws a helix.

Continuous rotation

Table moves, tube spins

Slip-ring technology lets the tube rotate continuously while the table feeds the patient through — the beam traces a helix along z.

Pitch

Feed per rotation ÷ beam width

Pitch 1: turns touch. Pitch > 1: gaps — faster, lower dose, interpolation fills in. Pitch < 1: overlap — more dose, more data.

Reconstruction

Interpolated planar slices

No single rotation lies in one plane, so axial images are interpolated from the helical dataset at any chosen position.

05 — The cone-beam effect

Only the centre
stays in plane.

With many detector rows the beam is no longer a flat fan but a cone. Central rows are hit almost perpendicular to z — but outer rows collect data from tilted, diverging planes.

As the gantry rotates, an outer row sweeps a double-cone through the patient rather than a flat slice.
The mismatch grows with wider arrays and shows as cone-beam artefact — corrected by dedicated reconstruction algorithms.

06 — Ring artefact

One bad detector,
one perfect circle.

In third-generation geometry each detector keeps a fixed distance from the isocentre as the gantry turns. A miscalibrated detector therefore writes its error along a circle — a ring in every image.

Watch the faulty channel sweep: every projection it contributes lies tangent to the same radius.
The fix is recalibration or interpolation across the faulty channel — and it distinguishes 3rd-gen from 4th-gen scanners in exams.
