T1 and T2 Signal

The MR signal is generated by nuclei precessing in-phase in the transverse (xy) plane, producing a net transverse magnetisation.

This net magnetisation precesses at the Larmor frequency, which induces an electrical voltage in the receiver coils. The detected signal is a sinusoidal waveform with the same frequency as the precessing nuclei.

The signal amplitude is greatest during and immediately after the 90° RF pulse is switched off.

After this:

  • The transverse magnetisation (Mxy) decays to zero.
  • The longitudinal magnetisation (Mz) recovers back to 100 percent.

These changes occur via two independent mechanisms:

  1. Spin-lattice relaxation
  2. Spin-spin relaxation

Both processes occur simultaneously but are completely independent. The recovery of Mz governed by T1 follows a different time course from the decay of Mxy governed by T2.

1. Spin-Lattice Relaxation

T1 Constant

As nuclei precess in the transverse plane, they interact with surrounding molecules, known as the lattice. During this interaction, energy is transferred from the nuclei to the lattice.

As energy is lost, magnetisation returns exponentially to the longitudinal axis (Mz). This process is called spin-lattice relaxation, also known as longitudinal relaxation.

The rate of this recovery is defined by the T1 time constant.

Definition of T1

T1 is the time required for Mz to recover to 63 percent of its maximum value.

T1 depends on the molecular environment and lattice properties.

T1 is always longer than T2, except in pure water where T1 equals T2.

Effect of the 90° RF Pulse

A 90° RF pulse rotates all longitudinal magnetisation (Mz) into the transverse plane (Mxy).

Therefore, a large Mz signal becomes an Mxy signal of equal magnitude after the pulse.

For now, we focus on Mz recovery from zero, but this principle becomes important when discussing weighted imaging.

Factors Affecting T1

Fat and protein
Have short T1 values. Their large molecules have low intrinsic energy and efficiently absorb energy from precessing nuclei. This leads to rapid loss of Mxy and quick recovery of Mz.

Water
Has a long T1. Small, rapidly moving molecules are inefficient at energy transfer, resulting in slower Mz recovery.

Bone, calcium, and metal
Have very long T1 values. Their rigid macromolecular structure makes them poor at removing energy from precessing nuclei.
